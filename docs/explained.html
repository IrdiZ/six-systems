What is this, actually?

In one sentence

We built a fake hospital to show that having every file about a patient is not the same as having the data, and we measured how big that gap is.

01 · THE PROBLEM"We only get partial data"

Doctors and researchers at hospitals keep saying versions of the same thing: departments can't share data about the same patient, and "we miss the raw data, we only get partial data."

That sounds strange at first, because modern hospitals are very digital. Every scan, lab result and report is stored. Open the patient's record and everything is there.

The catch is in the word there. It is there for a human to look at. It is often not there for a computer to use: to calculate with, compare over time, send to another hospital, or feed into research and AI.

03 · WHAT WE BUILTA fake hospital, on purpose

We can't use real patient data (and shouldn't, without permission). So we simulated it. We invented 20 people and sent them through realistic care: a heart scare, a lung spot that turns out to be a tumour, and slowly failing kidneys. Along the way they touch six different systems, and each system writes its files the way real ones do: in its own format, with its own habits and its own flaws.

Then we built a program that tries to do what a hospital would need: collect all those files, figure out which patient each belongs to, and turn them into clean data. Finally, we measured how much it managed, and exactly where and why the rest got stuck.

3 · VIEWERShows the gap

The interactive page: the archive view next to the data view, a score, and where every value came from.

The answer key. Because we invented the patients, we know every true value. The simulator writes them into a hidden answer key. The assembler never sees it. Afterwards we compare, fact by fact. That is what makes the score honest: it isn't "how much did we find?" but "how much of the truth survived?"

07 · THE RESULTThree numbers

The percentages reflect how this simulation was built. They show the mechanism, not a measured rate at any hospital.

The gap between the first two numbers is the whole story: the archive shows almost everything, but only part of it arrives as usable data. The third number shows the gap can be closed a long way, but only with a lot of translating, matching and reading, which is the work nobody currently owns.

08 · WHY IT MATTERSTwo different problems hiding in one complaint

Care between organisations. A patient's results from a neighbouring hospital or regional lab arrive with different codes, different units, sometimes no patient number, sometimes by fax. Doctors re-type, re-check, or miss them. The Dutch exchange law (the Wegiz) is on hold until it is aligned with EU rules. The EU's European Health Data Space (EHDS) now sets the clock: patient summaries by 2029, lab results and images by 2031.

Research and AI. Researchers need thousands of cases as clean data. But key measurements live inside images and PDFs, raw scanner data is thrown away, and every study re-does the same manual export. European rules (the EHDS) are starting to require this kind of reuse to be possible.

Same root cause, different owners, different fixes. The prototype exists to ask one question: which of the two hurts more in practice? That decides what is worth building next.

09 · WHAT THIS IS NOTBeing honest about it

  • Not a measured rate. We chose how often results get faxed, lack a patient number, and so on. The percentages follow from those choices.
  • Not real data. Every person, number and organisation is invented.
  • Not a product and not a medical device. It is a question in the form of software.
  • Not the real formats in full detail. Realistic in shape, simplified underneath.
  • Easier than reality. Reading numbers off images works well here because we drew the images ourselves with clean fonts. Real screens are messier, so real numbers would be worse.
  • Not any specific hospital's data flows. It models a typical Dutch academic hospital from public information.

10 · WORDS YOU'LL SEESmall glossary

Archive (PACS)
The system where hospitals store scans and images. Built for viewing, not for extracting data.
EHR
Electronic health record: the main patient file (at many Dutch academic hospitals, Epic).
HL7v2
An old but universal format hospital systems use to send results to each other internally.
EDIFACT
An even older text format labs still use to send results to GPs and other hospitals.
DICOM
The standard file format for medical images.
Screen capture
A DICOM file that is just a picture of a screen, e.g. the ultrasound machine's measurement panel.
BSN
The Dutch citizen number: the one ID that lets you match a patient across organisations.
OCR
Software that reads text out of an image. It makes mistakes: here it read "cm" as "¢m".
LOINC
A universal code for each lab test, so "Kreatinine" and "Creatinine" mean the same thing to a computer.
Pseudonymise
Replace identity with a code so records stay linkable for research but nobody can tell who they belong to.
